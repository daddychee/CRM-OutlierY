Consider the night sky. Out there, beyond the reach of any single eye, a hundred billion galaxies wheel in silence, each one a hundred billion suns. We are made of star-stuff, the iron in our blood forged in the heart of some ancient, exploded star. And yet here we are, on this small blue world, asking questions of the dark.

We have sent our fragile machines past the orbit of Neptune, out into the space between the stars. We have looked back, from four billion miles away, and seen our entire civilization as a single pale dot, suspended in a sunbeam. Every war, every triumph, every act of love and cruelty in human history happened there, on that mote of dust.

It is worth pausing, for a moment, to feel the scale of it. The universe is not required to be in harmony with human ambition. It does not care whether we understand it. And yet, against all odds, we have begun to understand a little — not because the cosmos owes us an answer, but because we kept asking.

So we look up, again and again, and we wonder. Not because wonder is comfortable, but because it is honest. The most astonishing fact, perhaps, is that the universe is comprehensible at all, that creatures evolved to find ripe fruit and avoid predators can, after a few thousand years of trying, begin to read the grammar of galaxies.
