Imagine standing on the shore of a cosmic ocean. For most of human history, we did not even know it was an ocean. We thought the lights overhead were small, fixed, close — lanterns hung just beyond the clouds. It took courage, and centuries of careful measurement, to discover that those lights were suns like our own, scattered across an almost incomprehensible distance.

We are, every one of us, a temporary arrangement of atoms that were once inside a star. Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, the calcium in our bones — none of it was made on Earth. It was cooked in stellar furnaces billions of years before our planet existed, then scattered across the galaxy by supernovae, only to be gathered again, here, into something that can look up and ask where it came from.

This should not make us feel small. It should make us feel connected — to the stars, to the deep past, to every other curious mind that has ever turned its attention skyward and tried to make sense of the dark between the lights.

And so the next time you step outside on a clear night, look up. You are not a visitor to the universe. You are a piece of it, briefly arranged so that it can wonder about itself.
