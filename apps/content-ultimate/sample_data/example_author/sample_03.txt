There is a kind of humility that comes from genuine knowledge, and a kind of arrogance that comes from its absence. The more we learn about the universe, the smaller our claims to centrality become — and the larger our sense of belonging grows.

We once believed the Earth was the unmoving center of all things, that the sun and stars revolved around us by necessity. Each correction to that picture felt, at the time, like a loss. It was not. It was an invitation to a bigger story: one galaxy among hundreds of billions, one star among hundreds of billions in that galaxy, one small rocky world among the orbiting debris of that star — and somehow, against staggering odds, a place where matter became aware of itself.

We should not look away from how strange this is. A universe vast enough to make us feel insignificant has also, on at least one small world, produced beings capable of measuring its age, mapping its structure, and asking what it all means. That capacity for wonder is not separate from the universe. It is the universe, examining itself.
